# Evidence collection

Use this file for workflow steps 1 to 4. Record each item with its source: `supplied`, or a source you read in this session.

## Fact-sheet interview

Ask the person who delivers the work, or the owner. Ask:

1. What do you do for the customer that the customer never sees?
2. What do you refuse to do that competitors do?
3. What did you change after a refund, a cancellation or a bad review?
4. Which customers get the best results with the least hand-holding, and what do they have in common?
5. What do buyers try first, and what do they say on the first call?
6. Which numbers do you track, such as refund rate, on-time rate or callbacks? Record only the numbers the owner gives; mark the rest `unknown`.

Record ordinary answers too. A step the owner calls obvious can be the attribute a buyer values.

## Buyer words

- Keep a term list of the words buyers use for the problem and the product, each quoted exactly with its source. The statement uses these words.
- List the owner's terms that buyers never use, each with the buyer's word for the same thing.

## Complaints

1. Read the 1-3 star reviews of two or three alternatives (heuristic default) and of the product. Use reviews the user supplied or pages you read in this session. Skip pages behind a login and say so.
2. Quote each repeated fear exactly from supplied text, with its source, and count the reviews that say it, supplied or read.
3. Take the top three repeated fears (heuristic default) as objections. For each, write the objection in the buyer's words when the text was supplied, paraphrased otherwise, the concern behind it (marked `assumed` unless the buyer said it), one response that the supplied proof backs, and the proof named. Label its type: price, timing, competition, authority, status quo, technical, or quality or trust. If no proof fits, say so and write `[PLACEHOLDER: proof needed]`.
4. Name the buyer profile where a rival fits better, in the buyer's terms, and keep that profile out of the segment. Do not state a buyer's claim about a rival as fact; quote it as the buyer's words.

## Alternatives

1. Show the status quo first as the baseline. Rank the other alternatives by how often customers name them.
2. Sort each alternative as direct (same solution, same problem), secondary (different solution, same problem) or indirect (a conflicting approach, such as paying a person to do the task).
3. Show each alternative's worst weakness in the buyer's words, quoted from supplied text, with its source. Each weakness is a candidate attribute.
4. For each alternative, note the switching forces in the supplied text: push (what drives buyers away from it), pull (what draws them to the product), habit (what keeps them on it) and anxiety (what worries them about switching). Mark a force `unknown` when no text shows it.

## Claim triage

Sort the claims that the alternatives make:

- **Saturated:** three or more alternatives make the claim, or the AI answers repeat it; an AI answer is a snapshot, not proof. Heuristic default: three.
- **Contested:** one or two alternatives make the claim. Use it only with more proof or specificity.
- **White space:** a fear buyers raise that no alternative answers with a specific promise. Use it only when complaints show the buyer cares and the product can keep the promise.

Saturated claims do not lead the position.

## AI answers

Run these prompt types on the AI assistants you can reach. Heuristic default: two assistants. If you cannot reach an assistant, request its pasted answer in Next.

- Category: "What is the best [category] for [buyer]?" and "How do I choose a [category]?" For a local business, the two prompts become: "Who is the best [category] company in [city]?" and "How do I choose a [category] in [city]?"
- Comparison: "[alternative A] vs [alternative B] for [buyer]."
- Brand: "What is [product] and who is it for?"
- Objection: "What are the downsides of [category]?"

Record for each answer:

- who is named first;
- each descriptor, paraphrased unless the user pasted the answer;
- the comparison axes the answer uses;
- whether the product appears, and with which descriptor;
- the warnings the answer volunteers.

Warnings are live objections; the proof must answer them. Answers change between runs, so one run is a snapshot. Do not argue against a consensus head-on: if every answer praises the same axis, own an axis the answers leave out. Before a repositioning, record one snapshot for the old segment's prompts and one for the new segment's prompts.
