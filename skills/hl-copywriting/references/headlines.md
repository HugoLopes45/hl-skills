# Headlines

Use this file to write or pick a headline. The headline states the one concept chosen in SKILL.md; variants change the headline and the line under it, never the concept.

A headline is usually found, not composed. Before writing one, reread the material list from [originality.md](originality.md): the buyer's sentence, the exact number, the limit, and the admitted flaw are each a headline waiting to be trimmed.

Examples use an invented mail-in knife sharpening service. Every number in the examples is invented and shows the pattern only.

## One headline

- Write one sentence, about 6 to 14 words (heuristic default), about the result the reader gets or the problem they name, per the lead, not the product's features. Use the immediate result for an urgent need and the later outcome for a planned one.
- Make it survive being read alone, with no image and no page around it.
- Test the line with the prefix "Now you can" in mind; the prefix is a mental test, never published. Keep the line only when the result is a new ability the reader gets and is true.
- Use the traffic source's words when the page answers that source.
- Cut a headline that names a category only, joins two promises with "and", or would fit a competitor's page unchanged.
- Cut a headline that only restates the brief's outcome line. The brief says what the product does; the headline says it the way the buyer would feel it.
- Put a year in a headline only when the brief says the page will be kept current for that year. Dated copy that outlives its date reads as abandoned.
- Clear before clever. A headline may surprise; it may not need solving. When a clever line hides what the product is, the line under it must say so at once, or the clever line goes.
- Curiosity needs a benefit attached. A line that only teases is skipped.
- A headline that contradicts a common belief must be paid off in the first section. A bold claim over ordinary content disappoints.
- The line under the headline does the other half: outcome above, mechanism below, or the reverse.

## Patterns

Write one variant per pattern the supplied facts can carry. Skip a pattern the facts cannot support.

1. **Outcome and timeframe:** "Get your knives back sharp in 5 working days." (invented)
2. **Named enemy:** "The drawer dulls the knife you use most." (invented)
3. **Specific number:** "How 212 home cooks keep one set sharp for a year." (invented)
4. **Transformation:** "From a drawer of dull knives to a set you trust." (invented)
5. **Customer quote:** the buyer's own sentence from a supplied source, cut by whole sentences only and never reworded, with its source named. Never write a quote. A confession or a complaint makes a stronger headline than praise.
6. **Admitted flaw:** "It takes five days. Good edges do." (invented) The supplied flaw, then its reason.
7. **The limit:** "We sharpen knives. We do not sharpen serrated blades. Each one is ground to the angle on its label." (invented) What the product refuses, when the brief supplies it, and a supplied fact as the last item.
8. **Plain statement of the odd fact:** "Every knife leaves here checked under a 10x loupe." (invented) No device; the fact is strange enough.
9. **How to, without the pain:** "How to keep one good knife sharp without learning to use a stone." (invented)
10. **The reason why:** "Why the knife you use most is the dullest one you own." (invented) Only when the body gives the reason.
11. **The buyer's question:** the question the buyer asked in a supplied source or in the traffic source, answered by the line under it.
12. **The moment:** "Sunday, 6 pm, and the tomato wins again." (invented) A time and place of use from the material list; the line under it must name the product.
13. **Category for audience:** "Mail-in sharpening for cooks with one good knife." (invented) The audience is the buyer's own term from a supplied source, and the line passes the swap test.
14. **Turn input into outcome:** "Turn a dull drawer knife into one you reach for." (invented) Both the input and the outcome come from supplied facts.
15. **Permission:** "You can start with one dull knife." (invented) For a reader who doubts they qualify. Use only a supplied eligibility or limit.
16. **Assessment:** "Find out which of three edge problems your knife has." (invented) Use only when the brief supplies the assessment and its classes; the headline promises that assessment.

Patterns 6, 7, and 12 are where humor most often lives; run the gate in [humor.md](humor.md) first.

## Variants and pick

- All variants stay inside the one concept. List the best three (heuristic default); the rest stay unshown.
- Mark one as the pick and give one line of why: the reader it fits and the source of its words.

```
Pick: "Get your knives back sharp in 5 working days." (invented)
Why: it names the outcome and the turnaround a buyer compares.
```

Ordinary draft reports list the variants after the copy, with the pick first. Copy-only requests list them only when asked.
