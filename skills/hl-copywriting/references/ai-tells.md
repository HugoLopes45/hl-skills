# Clarity and specificity review

Use this file for the pattern check and rewrite pass. Patterns are prompts to inspect meaning, not evidence of authorship. Draft bans apply to new copy; in a rewrite, keep the author's explicit negations, contrasts, and exclusions as claims. Avoiding a pattern does not make a line specific or true.

## Claims first

- Replace unsupported authority, customer counts, testimonials and guarantees with supplied evidence, or leave them out; a placeholder only in the two cases under "Placeholders are rare" in SKILL.md. Follow [proof-and-claims.md](proof-and-claims.md).
- Replace vague benefits with the supported action, outcome or limitation. Do not invent a measurement to make a line sound specific.
- Preserve both sides of meaningful contrasts, explicit exclusions, conditions and negations. Remove a rhetorical foil only when it carries no intended claim.
- Preserve the author's opinions as opinions. A feeling need not be converted into a measurable product benefit.

## Draft bans

Apply these to new copy.

- **Contrast reveal and negation list.** Do not write "Not X. Y." when X is a foil nobody claimed; state Y directly. A contrast against something the brief or the buyer actually says is allowed, one per piece (heuristic default). Do not write "No X, no Y, no Z." Keep one supplied absence, such as "no setup fee", next to the call to action when it matters.
- **Trailing pile-on.** End the sentence at the claim. A comma clause must add a fact the reader can check; cut a clause that only restates.
- **Balancing clause.** Cut "That said," and "However, it's important" after a stated opinion. Keep the opinion, or add the supplied limit that qualifies it.
- **Colon or self-answered question.** Do not write "The best part: it learns." or "The result? 3x faster." Write the sentence. A question the buyer would ask, and an FAQ question, stay.
- **Vague word.** Replace "seamless", "robust", "powerful", "streamline", and "empower" with the fact each stands for. A synonym swap keeps the vagueness.
- **Filler and wordy verbs.** Cut "very", "really", "just", "actually", and "in order to" (use "to"). Prefer "use" to "utilise", "start" to "commence", and "buy" to "purchase".
- **Em dash.** Use none in headlines, subheads, ads, social posts, or subject lines.
- **Stock phrase.** Replace "Say goodbye to", "Unlock the power of", "Game-changer", "all-in-one", "Effortless", and "Join thousands of happy customers" with the outcome, the step, or the supplied count.
- **Assistant register.** Cut "Great question!", "I hope this helps!", "Happy to help!", and "As an AI". Cut "Enter [product]." and "Think of it as [analogy]." Keep an analogy only when it shows a supplied fact.
- **Weasel source.** Replace "experts agree", "studies show", and "trusted by industry leaders" with the named source, or cut the claim.
- **Formatting.** Use no emoji bullets and no exclamation points in body copy. Use no decorative unicode in body copy: arrows, multiplication signs, styled letters. Use sentence case for headings unless the brand guide says otherwise.
- **Second-generation tell.** Delete a line that comments on the copy ("Here's why this matters.") and a line that states an insight nobody would dispute ("Speed is a feature."). Replace it with the fact it stands for, or cut it. End a section on a fact, a number, or the call to action, not a tidy moral.
- **Overreach.** Narrow a claim that is wider than its supplied evidence to what the evidence shows.
- **Candor promise.** Cut "Here's the thing", "Let me be clear", "The truth is", and "Here's what most people miss". They promise frankness and delay it. Say the frank thing.
- **Label heading.** A heading that names its section ("How it works", "The problem", "Pricing", "Why us", "Three steps") becomes the claim that section makes, or goes.
- **Echo line.** A sentence that repeats a brief line in the brief's own words and order ("The service does not work with paper invoices.") informs and does not sell. Rebuild it from the reader's side, or move it to a facts list.
- **Answer that repeats its question.** "Does it work with X? No. It does not work with X." Give the answer once, then the reason or the way around.
- **Importance puffery.** Cut "pivotal", "redefines", "revolutionary", "next-generation", and "more than just a". State the fact.
- **Verb dodge.** Replace "serves as", "boasts", "offers", "provides", "enables", and "is designed to" with the verb for what the product does.
- **False range.** Replace "from freelancers to enterprises" and "whether you're X or Y" with the buyer the brief names.
- **Hedge stack.** "Can help you potentially reduce" becomes what is known, or the claim scoped to its evidence. One qualifier per claim (heuristic default).
- **Quiet-register filler.** "Quietly", "simply", "it matters", "that lands", "do the work", "real" and "honest" used as sincerity markers carry nothing. Cut them.
- **Performed cleverness.** A neat pairing or reversal built to sound smart ("Work smarter, sharpen harder") is kept only when it states a supplied fact and the voice is playful. See [humor.md](humor.md).

## Pattern check

Run this on every draft before delivery. Each heuristic default is a review prompt, not a fact.

1. Search the draft for each pattern in Draft bans. Check every hit against its rule.
2. Read each sentence over 20 words (heuristic default) that has more than two commas. Split it, or cut the trailing clause.
3. Keep one idea per sentence. Keep web paragraphs to four sentences at most (heuristic default), and vary them: see Rhythm. Use active verbs, not nouns made from verbs ("decide", not "make a decision").
4. Keep at most one fragment run and one list of three per piece (heuristic default). Keep a list of three only when its three items are distinct facts, not padding.
5. Apply the stacking test: two or more hits in one paragraph (heuristic default) means rewrite that paragraph from its facts.
6. Run the shape check in Rhythm on the whole piece.
7. Vary the fix. Do not repair every hit with the same new construction; a repeated repair is a new pattern.

## Rhythm

Uniform shape is the strongest sign that nobody wrote the piece. Check the whole piece, not single lines.

- **Sentence length.** Sentences of about the same length in a row read as a list. Give each sentence the length its idea needs: a short one after a long one lands; three short ones in a row is a drumbeat.
- **Paragraph length.** Not every paragraph is two or three sentences. A one-line paragraph, then a longer one, is how a person writes.
- **Section balance.** When every section has a heading, two sentences, and three bullets, rewrite. The section with the most material runs longest; a section with one thing to say is one line.
- **Devices are rationed.** One list of three, one fragment run, one contrast, and one or two lines built to be remembered per piece (heuristic default). The second use of a device weakens the first.
- **Bullets.** Bullets that all open with a bold label, or all with the same verb form, become sentences, or open with what the reader gets.
- **Parallel pairs.** Two short matching phrases ("Heavy on purpose. Hard to cut.") are the default shape of generated headlines and taglines. Keep the pair only when the second phrase adds a fact the first does not.
- **Endings.** Sections that each end on a short punchy line form a pattern. End most sections on the fact.
- **Read it aloud.** Mark each place where you would not say the sentence to one buyer across a table. Rewrite those.

## Review candidates

Inspect these only when they hide meaning, add no useful information or conflict with the requested voice:

- Stock openings (see Openings and flow).
- A paragraph that ends without a detail, question, or promise that the next paragraph answers, or an opened loop left open.
- Momentum killers: jargon, wall-of-text paragraphs, tangents, and back-to-back sentences with the same shape.
- Grand claims without an explanation of what the product does.
- Rhetorical questions whose answers merely repeat the headline.
- Lists or fragments padded to create a rhythm rather than convey distinct facts.
- Repeated benefit summaries without new information.
- Abstract subjects and verbs that hide who does what.
- Synonym cycling that gives one concept several names.
- Decorative formatting that makes the requested artifact harder to use.

Keep a question the reader needs answered, a contrast that expresses the offer, or repetition that aids comprehension. Follow the user's punctuation and brand rules. Do not introduce mistakes to appear human, or replace one vague adjective with another.

## Openings and flow

- Open each piece with a short first sentence that names the subject or a concrete fact.
- Cut stock openers such as "In a world where", "Are you tired of", "Whether you're a ... or a ...", "Let's explore", and "Imagine a world" when they delay the subject.
- Cut transitions such as "moreover", "furthermore", and "in conclusion". The order of the sentences shows the link. Do not repeat one connector across a page, and do not add one to every sentence.

## Check the artifact

1. Trace every changed claim to the brief or existing copy. Restore lost facts, scope and voice.
2. Read each sentence in context. Explain an edit as clearer, more specific, better supported or better matched to the reader, never merely less AI-like.
3. Check whether a competitor could use the same claim unchanged. Where differentiation matters, use a supported distinction or mark the evidence gap.
4. Confirm each number's unit, period and basis where relevant. Keep unsupported proof outside publishable copy.
5. Deliver the requested artifact without expanding a narrow revision into a new campaign.
