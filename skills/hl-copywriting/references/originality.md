# Originality

Use this file before the first sentence of a draft, and in a rewrite when a line could belong to any company. Originality here is not decoration. It is the use of material that only this product has, arranged around one idea.

Examples use an invented mail-in knife sharpening service. Every detail in the examples is invented and shows the method only. Counts in this file (three concepts, one or two remembered lines) are heuristic defaults.

## Why copy comes out generic

A writer who starts from the product category writes the category's page: the average of every page already written for it. A writer who starts from the brief's oddest facts cannot. Start from the material, never from the format's usual sentences.

## Mine the material

Read the brief and every supplied source once for facts, then once more for these. Copy each find exactly, with its source line.

1. **The number that is not round.** "71 days", "4 minutes 10 seconds", "1.4 kg". An exact figure reads as lived; a rounded one reads as marketing. Never round a supplied number to make it tidy.
2. **The thing the product does not do.** A stated limit ("does not sharpen serrated blades") is rarer than a feature and more believed. It often makes the strongest line on the page.
3. **The flaw the owner admits.** Slow, heavy, plain, expensive, small. A flaw that exists for a reason is a claim: say the flaw, then the reason.
4. **The sentence a buyer said.** Look for the confession, the complaint, or the odd comparison, not the praise. "I've been cutting tomatoes with the bread knife for a year" beats "great service".
5. **The founder's moment.** A dated, specific event that started the product. Use it only as supplied.
6. **The step the owner calls obvious.** The routine thing the business does that competitors also do and nobody explains. The first to describe it plainly owns it.
7. **The enemy.** The habit, the workaround, or the alternative the buyer uses now. An enemy is a thing, never a named competitor unless the brief supplies the comparison.
8. **The moment of use.** When and where the buyer meets the problem: a day, a room, an hour. A time and a place turn a benefit into a scene.
9. **The gap.** What the buyer says they do against what they do. Most humor and most recognition live here.
10. **Two supplied numbers that argue when put side by side.** A buyer who cut tomatoes with the bread knife for a year and a service that returns the knife in 5 working days make the case with no new claim. Set them side by side and stop: no "so", "because", or result joins them without a brief line. Look across the brief's sections for pairs like this; briefs file them far apart.

When this list comes back empty, do not fill it with invention. Write the short, plain version, say in line 1 that the brief holds no distinguishing material, and put in Next the questions that would produce some:

- What do customers say in the first minute after it works?
- What do you refuse to do that competitors do?
- What do people get wrong about it before they buy?
- What is the most common complaint, and is it deliberate?
- What happened the day you decided to build it?

## Find the concept

A concept is the single thing the piece says, stated in one sentence with the fact that makes it true. Words come after.

- Write three (heuristic default). Each must make a different claim. "Sharp knives in 5 days" and "Your knives back sharp within a week" are one concept written twice.
- A usable concept joins a buyer truth to a product fact: "You already own good knives; they are just dull" joins the drawer of unused knives to a service that restores them.
- Try at least one concept built on a limit or a flaw, and one built on a buyer sentence. They are the ones the category has not used.
- Choose by two tests: a competitor could not run it unchanged, and the supplied facts can carry it down the whole piece. A clever concept with one supporting fact dies after the headline.
- One concept per piece. The headline states it, one section proves it, and the last line returns to it. Every other section serves it or goes.

## Tests for a line

- **Swap test.** Put a competitor's name on the line. If it still reads as true, the line is generic. Fix it with a find from the material list, not with a stronger adjective.
- **Only-here test.** Underline the words that come from this brief and no other. A paragraph with nothing underlined is filler.
- **Friend test.** Could a buyer repeat the line to a friend the same evening, without the page? If it needs the page, it is too clever or too vague.
- **Byline test.** Remove the brand name. Does the piece still sound like one particular company?
- **Clear before clever.** If the reader must solve the line to get the fact, the line fails. A line can be surprising and instantly understood; it cannot be a riddle.

## Dead and alive, from the same facts

Invented brief: a mail-in knife sharpening service; 5 working days; 12 EUR per knife; ground to the angle on the knife's label; does not sharpen serrated blades; founder's note "most knives we get were never bad, they lived in a drawer"; one buyer email "I've been cutting tomatoes with the bread knife for a year".

Dead version. Every sentence is supported, and nobody would read it:

```
## Professional knife sharpening by mail
We sharpen your knives and return them in 5 working days.
## How it works
1. Mail us your knives. 2. We sharpen them. 3. We return them.
## Pricing
12 EUR per knife.
## FAQ
Do you sharpen serrated blades? No. We do not sharpen serrated blades.
```

It fails the swap test (any sharpener), the echo test (the brief, reformatted), and the label-heading rule. The buyer's email and the founder's note, the two best things in the brief, are missing.

Alive version, same facts and no new ones:

```
## Your good knife is not dead. It has been living in a drawer.
One buyer emailed us: "I've been cutting tomatoes with the bread knife
for a year." Of course you have.
Most knives we get were never bad. They lived in a drawer.

## Post it to us. It is back in 5 working days.
We grind each knife to the angle printed on its own label. 12 EUR a knife.

## Serrated blades stay home
We do not sharpen serrated blades. Keep those out of the box.
```

What changed: the strongest find (the buyer's email) moved to the first screen; the founder's note became the concept ("never bad, they lived in a drawer"); the limit became the closing line instead of an FAQ echo; each heading makes a claim; the middle section, where the method is, carries the fact a buyer checks. Nothing is stated that the brief does not hold.

## Build the writer

Decide who is speaking before writing: the founder, the person at the bench, the support lead. Take the choice from the supplied voice evidence. Then write as that one person.

- The writer knows some part of the product cold. That section runs longest and carries the most detail. Sections of equal length and equal shape read as assembled, not written.
- The writer has a stance the owner supplied or the founder's note shows. A stance someone could disagree with ("a knife block is where edges go to die") is a voice. "We believe in quality" is not, because nobody believes the opposite.
- The writer says what the product is not for, once, plainly.
- The writer uses first person only where it adds a fact the reader can check.
- Without voice evidence, do not perform a personality: write plainly, treat a tempting light line as a close call under humor.md, say in the notes that the voice is unverified, and let the material carry the piece.

## Punch is rationed

A piece gets one or two lines built to be remembered (heuristic default), usually the headline and one more. Every other line does its job quietly. A page where each sentence is a device (a contrast, a three-part list, a kicker) reads as generated, however good each device is alone.

## One name per thing

Pick the buyer's word for each thing and keep it. Do not rotate synonyms for variety. Coin a term only when the product has a real, named part and the piece will define it once and reuse it; "our proven process" names nothing.

## Where not to be original

Price, terms, legal text, instructions, destructive confirmations, and errors that block the reader are written literally. In these places the original thing is being clearer than anyone else.
