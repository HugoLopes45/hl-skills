# Voice and audience

Use this file to choose the register of the copy for the audience the brief names. Apply the same rules to any market, language, or age group.

## Choose the register

1. Collect the words the buyer uses: reviews, support tickets, complaints, cancellation reasons, comments, forum threads, interviews, sales calls. Copy three to five phrases exactly, with their source and locale; the count is a heuristic default.
2. Put those phrases into the headline, subhead, and problem section where they fit the claim.
3. Without supplied words, write plain, direct sentences, and say so in the notes, as SKILL.md sets.
4. Thin evidence is still evidence. One or two supplied signals (two reviews, a founder note, a line of existing copy) set the direction of the voice; use them, say in the notes that the voice rests on them, and keep the dose small. Do not discard a supplied signal because there are fewer than three.
5. Weigh sources: how the founder or the team writes unprompted (support replies, notes, posts) outranks polished site copy. When the two conflict, write to the unprompted one and say so.
6. Practitioner jargon the buyer uses signals a peer; keep it. Aspiration words the buyer never uses signal a seller; cut them.
7. Write in the language of the brief. Do not translate buyer phrases into a second language unless the brief asks for it.
8. Keep one register across the piece.
9. Hold the voice constant across contexts; set its intensity per context. Errors and support text follow [microcopy.md](microcopy.md). Billing, legal, and incident text: exact numbers and dates, no humor.
10. When the user asks for a voice guide, keep three lists: terms the product owns, the buyer's words, and words the copy never uses. Give each never-use word its reason.

## Voice check

Use this when the brief names a brand voice or supplies copy to match.

1. Pin each voice claim to a verbatim exhibit of 5 to 60 words from supplied copy (heuristic default). A claim with no exhibit stays unverified.
2. Ask the owner for an anti-model: one voice to avoid, and the phrases in it that grate. Ask for the belief about the category that competitors would not say, samples the owner trusts, and hard lines (profanity, humor, hot takes, emoji). A voice built only from these answers stays unverified.
3. Build the voice from supplied copy that performed, when the owner gives the results.
4. Give each attribute a "does not mean" note and one supplied line that shows it. "Direct" does not mean curt.
5. Turn each attribute into behavior a reader could observe. "Confident" becomes "states figures without hedges and opens with the conclusion". "Funny" must say what kind: dry understatement, self-mockery about the company, or exact detail.
6. When a writing sample is supplied, match its sentence length, punctuation habits, paragraph openings, and pet words. The sample outranks any default in this skill, and the owner's edits of earlier drafts outrank the owner's own description of the voice.
7. A mild voice is a valid finding when stated concretely ("no humor and no first person in the supplied pages"). Vague is not a voice.
8. When the user requests a voice guide, test the voice: write three samples (heuristic default) in this skill's formats: a first-screen headline with its supporting line, a button with the friction line under it, and an error message. Ask the owner to react and correct the attributes, and repeat until the owner accepts the voice. After three rounds (heuristic default) without acceptance, let the owner choose one of three moves: deliver the best version, one targeted round, or extract the voice from copy the owner trusts. Put an unlabeled draft beside a competitor's supplied copy; if the owner cannot tell which is theirs, the voice is too generic.
9. In a rewrite to a supplied voice, list each drifting line beside the sample line it breaks among the changed lines; report drift only with both quotes. A drift that repeats across pieces may be a voice change; ask the owner before updating the voice.

## Choose the lead

Set the lead from the buyer's awareness level. Take the level from the brief or buyer sources; without them, label it unverified.

- Unaware: lead with identity or a supplied story, not the product; without one, lead with the buyer's problem in their words.
- Problem-aware: name the problem in the buyer's words, then say that a fix exists.
- Solution-aware: lead with the mechanism that differs from the alternatives.
- Product-aware: lead with the differentiator and its proof.
- Most aware: lead with the offer.
- A buyer who holds a belief the copy contradicts: name the belief in the buyer's words before the claim. Do not argue against it head-on.

## Culture-native, not costumed

- Culture-native copy sounds like the buyer's own community: the same formality and the same shared references.
- Do not add slang, memes, emoji, or lowercase styling to signal a generation or a platform. Readers spot imitation, and it lowers trust.
- Humor is decided with the gate in [humor.md](humor.md): the voice must allow it, the moment must allow it, and the material must supply it (a supplied fact, or the product's own world). It is never at the buyer's expense.
- Use platform jargon only when a supplied source uses it in the same sense.

## Audience labels

- An age group, generation, or platform habit is a hypothesis about a segment, not a fact about one buyer.
- Name a segment only when the brief names it. Confirm the segment with the buyer's own words before you write to it.
- Do not infer where a buyer discovers products, or what they trust, from a segment label. Use a source the user supplied, or write unverified.
- Two segments that weigh risk differently: write one section or page per segment. Do not average a claim that one segment reads as a red flag.

## Modern agency voice

- Write like a current brand that talks with its audience, not at it: direct, specific, and free of corporate filler. Modern describes the wording, not a generation: do not presume the customer is Gen Z or any other age group.
- Make the reader the subject of the sentence. Show the action they take, not the product's ambition.
- Ask a question only when the reader would ask it. Answer it in the next sentence.
- Turn each feature into a so-what chain: ask "so what?" of the feature, then of each answer, until the line reaches a time, money, or worry effect that the supplied facts support; where none is supplied, write the copy from the last supported line.
- Keep a formal brand's register. A formal sentence with a specific claim stays formal; do not clip it into fragments.
- Give the section the owner knows best the most words. Use an opinion only when the owner supplied it or the founder's own note states it.

## Creators and community

- Name a creator only with the creator's permission and an approved link. Without both, leave the creator out; write `[PLACEHOLDER: creator name and approved post link]` only when the user asked for the creator line.
- Write a founder story only from the founder's supplied story. Order it: the struggle first, then the credibility, then the result and the offer.
- Never write a creator quote, endorsement, or post that the user did not supply.
- Invite a community action (ask a question, post a result, join a thread) only when that community exists. Otherwise leave the invitation out, or use a placeholder for the link when the user asked for the invitation.
- A buyer who checks comments or reviews before buying needs proof they can check: a demo, a trial, a named customer quote with permission, or a published policy.

## Proof-led conversion

- Place the call to action next to the strongest supported proof it depends on.
- Replace each adjective with a check the buyer can run, from supplied facts: a demo, a trial, a measured result with its method, or a published policy; otherwise leave the adjective out.
