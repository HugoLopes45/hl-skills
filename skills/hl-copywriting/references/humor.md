# Humor

Use this file in draft step 4, and whenever a request asks for copy that is funny, playful, charming, or dry. Humor is a tool with a job: it makes a true thing stick and shows a person wrote it. It is decided per piece and per moment.

Examples use an invented mail-in knife sharpening service. Every detail in the examples is invented and shows the method only.

## The gate

Answer three questions in order. One "no" means the line is written straight.

1. **Does the voice allow it?** Yes when supplied copy, reviews, or the owner show it (a review calling the product "charming", a founder who writes dry support replies, a request for a playful tone). No when the owner rules it out. With no voice evidence at all, the answer is "dry at most": plain truth and understatement only, no wordplay; a tempting light line is a close call, handled under "When it is a close call".
2. **Does the moment allow it?** See the table below. The moment outranks the voice: a playful brand still writes a declined-card message straight.
3. **Does the material supply it?** Humor here comes from something true: a flaw, a limit, an exact number, a buyer's confession, the gap between what people say and do, or the product's own world (a plant app may speak of soil, light, and overwatering; a lock may speak of weight). Before answering "no", try the first four mechanisms below on each item of the material list. The material is there more often than not. Never invent a fact, a customer, a number, or an event to set a joke up.

When all three answers are "yes", write the line. In a "yes" moment, for a brand whose buyers call it charming, a flat line is as wrong as a joke on a payment error: it wastes the one place the voice could live.

One or two thin voice signals are enough to pass question 1 (heuristic default). They are not enough to turn the whole piece into a routine: use one light line in a low-stakes spot, and say in the notes what evidence the tone rests on.

## Moments

| Moment | Humor |
|---|---|
| 404 page, empty state, first-run screen, loading text seen once | Yes, when the gate passes. Nothing is at stake and the reader has a spare second. |
| About page, founder story, product description, tagline, packaging | Yes, when the gate passes. Personality is part of the job. |
| Success after a small action, thank-you page | One light touch. Confirm the fact first. |
| Headline of a landing page | Only when the joke is the claim itself. A reader who does not smile must still get the offer. |
| Body of a page: the problem as the buyer lives it, the limits, the founder's lines, the "not for you" line | Yes, when the gate passes. This is where a buyer's own rueful sentence, or an admitted limit, earns one dry line in reply. |
| Price, plan terms, what the buyer gets | No. Say it exactly. A light line may sit after the facts, never among them. |
| Buttons, labels, navigation, anything read every day | No. A joke read for the tenth time is an obstacle. |
| Payment failure, lost data, an outage, a blocking error, a security or privacy notice | No. The reader is blocked or worried. |
| Deleting, cancelling, refunds, anything that cannot be undone | No. |
| Health, safety, money at risk, legal or regulated claims, grief, job loss | No. |
| An apology, a price increase, a breach of a promise | No. Humor here reads as not caring. |
| A reader who is likely angry, or a buyer in an emergency | No. |

When the request asks for humor in a "no" moment, write the line straight, say in one sentence why, and offer the nearest "yes" moment in the same flow for the personality (the confirmation after the card is fixed, not the decline message).

## What may be the target

The product, the company, the founder, the category's clichés, the situation everyone shares. Never the buyer, never a group of people, never a named competitor. A joke about the buyer's mistake ("someone forgot to water again") is at the buyer's expense even when it is gentle. The same fact aimed at the plant or at the app is not. A buyer's own supplied sentence that laughs at their situation ("I'd rather lose the money than write that email") may be quoted and answered with sympathy: the reader is laughing with someone like them, and the copy sides with them.

Humor must not undercut the claim the buyer is paying for. A lock may joke about its weight, never about theft. A payroll tool may joke about spreadsheets, never about paying people correctly.

## Mechanisms

Each one works from a supplied fact. Try the first four before any other.

1. **The plain truth nobody says.** State the awkward fact flatly. "We sharpen knives. That is the entire company."
2. **The admitted flaw, then its reason.** "It takes five days. The post is slow and we refuse to rush an edge."
3. **Understatement.** Say less than the fact deserves. "A dull knife is mostly a disappointment. Occasionally it is the reason dinner runs late, and that part is not funny." (the second sentence turns straight on purpose)
4. **The over-exact detail.** Precision where nobody expects it. "Ground to 17 degrees, checked under a 10x loupe by someone who cares more than is healthy."
5. **The buyer's own confession, quoted.** A supplied sentence, placed where the reader recognizes themselves. Never written by you.
6. **The literal reading.** Take a category cliché at its word. "Lifetime sharpness" does not exist; "sharp until you use it, like every knife" is true.
7. **The short list with a small true last item.** Two real benefits and one modest, honest one. The third must be a fact, not a gag.
8. **The limit as a punchline.** "We do not sharpen scissors. We have tried. It went badly." Only when the owner supplied the limit and the story.
9. **The callback.** Return once, at the end, to a detail from the opening. It rewards the reader who finished.
10. **The company laughing at itself.** Size, obsession, an odd policy. Only from supplied facts about the company.

Stock puns unrelated to the product, references to films or songs, memes, and invented mascots are not on this list; they age fast, fail in translation, and are the first thing a generated page reaches for.

Wordplay drawn from the product's own world is allowed on low-stakes screens when the line still states the situation.

## Tests

Run all six on every humorous line.

- **Cut-the-joke test.** Remove the funny words. The sentence must still deliver a fact the reader needs. If nothing is left, put the fact first and the light line after it, or drop the line.
- **Straight-reader test.** A reader who takes it literally must not be misled. Irony that states the opposite of the truth fails in copy.
- **Tenth-read test.** Will it be seen repeatedly? Then write it straight.
- **Bad-day test.** Read it as someone who is tired, late, or annoyed with the product. If it grates, write it straight.
- **Translation test.** Will the copy be localized or read by non-native speakers? Keep humor of situation and fact; drop wordplay and idiom.
- **Supplied-fact test.** Name the brief line the joke stands on. No line, no joke.

## Dose and delivery

- One humorous line per screen or section, and at most two or three in a page (heuristic default). The rest of the copy is straight, which is what makes the line land.
- Fact first, lightness second, in any line that also carries information.
- Dry beats loud. No exclamation mark, no emoji as a laugh track, no "see what we did there", no line that explains the joke.
- Keep the sentence short. The surprise goes at the end of the sentence.
- Never open an error or an apology with a cute interjection ("Oops", "Uh oh", "Whoops").
- Keep one sense of humor across the piece. Dry in the headline and zany in the footer reads as two writers.

## When it is a close call

Deliver the straight line as the copy, and one humorous variant of the same line after the notes, labelled as a variant, with the fact it stands on. The owner knows the brand; the owner picks.

## Failure modes

- Charm instead of information: the empty state is witty and never says what to do next.
- Whimsy on a serious screen, because the brand is "fun".
- The same joke shape in every section.
- A joke that needs a fact the brief does not have.
- Sarcasm aimed at the reader's situation.
- A pun in a headline that hides what the product is.
- Humor that apologizes for the product ("we know, another app").
- A tone the support team could not keep up in a real reply.
