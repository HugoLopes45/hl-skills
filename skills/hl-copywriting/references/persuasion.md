# Persuasion

Use this file in draft step 5 when the piece must move a reader to act. Each move below works by helping the reader decide with true information. Each one has a dishonest twin, listed last, that this skill does not write.

Counts in this file are heuristic defaults. Every example line is invented and shows the pattern only.

## The reader decides in this order

1. Is this for me? (the first screen, in the reader's words)
2. What do I get? (the outcome, as a scene or a checkable fact)
3. How does it work? (the mechanism, short)
4. Why believe it? (proof beside the claim, or an honest limit)
5. What does it cost me? (money, time, effort, risk)
6. What do I do now? (one action, one label)

A piece that skips a question the reader still has stalls there. A piece that answers a question the reader no longer has wastes their attention. The buyer's awareness level in [voice-and-audience.md](voice-and-audience.md) says where to start.

## Moves

- **Before, then after.** State the present state clearly so the change is visible. Use the buyer's own description of the before; show the after as a supplied moment, not an adjective; otherwise leave the after out.
- **Immediate over distant.** Lead with what changes today or this week. A return a year away comes second and only with a supplied basis.
- **The reason why.** Give the supplied reason for a claim, a price, a limit, or a deadline; otherwise leave the reason out. A claim with its reason is believed more than a bigger claim without one.
- **The mechanism.** When competitors make the same bare promise, the promise alone reads as hype. Lead with how this product delivers it, in two sentences (heuristic default): what it does, then why that works, from a supplied reason; otherwise leave the second sentence out.
- **The admitted weakness.** One real limit, stated plainly near the offer, raises trust in every other line. It must be true and it must matter; a fake modest flaw is worse than none.
- **The cost of waiting.** When waiting has a real, supplied cost, state what the reader loses by waiting, in their units. With no real cost, write no urgency.
- **Risk reversal.** State a supplied guarantee, trial, or cancellation term next to the action, as one sentence the business would sign: the number, the window, what happens on a miss.
- **A smaller first step.** When the full commitment stops the click, offer a smaller step the brief supplies: a sample, a quote, a trial, a result shown before any form field.
- **Switching made small.** A reader who already uses something else fears the move more than the price. Show the switch as the supplied steps and what carries over.
- **Each doubt answered where it arises.** List every reason a reader might hesitate; answer each next to the line that raises it, and the strongest one beside the action.
- **Who it is not for.** One honest exclusion makes the inclusion credible and spares both sides a bad sale.
- **Give first.** When the brief supplies something useful to give before asking (a tool, a check, a sample), put it before the ask.
- **Fewer choices.** Each added option slows the decision. Present the choices the brief has; do not add any, and do not hide any.
- **Honest framing.** The same true fact may be stated from the side the reader cares about ("paid 11 days sooner" rather than "11 days less waiting") when both statements are equally true and complete.
- **The reader's own progress.** In a multi-step flow, show real progress and what is left. Never a false "almost done".
- **A good ending.** The last screen of a flow, the confirmation, and the first message after it are remembered. Give them the same care as the first screen.

## Keeping the reader reading

- Each element has one job: the headline gets the next line read, that line gets the first paragraph read, and so on down to the action.
- One point per paragraph. Two points are two paragraphs.
- Link paragraphs by cause, contrast, or consequence. Start some with "And", "So", or "But" when that is how a person would say it; not every one.
- Occasionally open a paragraph by repeating one key word from the end of the previous one, when the repeat carries the same supplied fact.
- End a paragraph, now and then, on something specific the next one answers. Two to four times in a page (heuristic default), not at every break.
- At most one question to the reader per page (heuristic default), and only one the reader is already asking.

## What this skill does not write

- Invented scarcity, countdowns, or deadlines.
- Reference prices, strike-throughs, or "value" totals the business never charged.
- Decoy plans, pre-selected paid options, or a plan badge the brief does not support.
- Guilt wording on a decline or cancel option.
- Fake limitations, fake reviews, fake counts of people viewing or buying.
- A threat of loss the product does not cause.
- Fear about health, safety, or money beyond what the supplied facts state.

When the user asks for one of these, decline that line, say why in one sentence, and write the honest move from the list above that does the same job.
