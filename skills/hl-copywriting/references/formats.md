# Formats

Use this file in draft step 5 to get the job, the shape, and the traps of the requested format. Read only the section you need. Each shape is a default that the material bends: a part with nothing true to say is left out.

Counts, lengths, and durations in this file are heuristic defaults. Every example line is invented and shows the pattern only.

## Rules for every page

- Headings state claims. Read the headings alone: they must make the argument in order.
- The first screen says what it is, who it is for, and the next step. A stranger gets all three at a glance, about five seconds (heuristic default).
- One page, one promise, one action. A second path is a quieter link, never a competing offer.
- One action has one label. Use the same button wording each time it appears; the line around it may change.
- The button names the action and what the reader gets ("Get my quote"). "Submit", "Learn more", and "Get started" say neither.
- One line under the button answers the doubt most likely to stop the click, from supplied facts: the cost, the commitment, or the effort.
- Proof sits beside the claim it supports; a separate proof section only when supplied proof is more than one item.
- If the headline states the outcome, the line under it states how; if the headline states how, the line under it states the outcome. Never restate the headline in longer words.

## Landing page

Job: one visitor from one source takes one action.

Shape: first screen (headline, one line, button, friction line); the problem as the buyer lives it; how it works; proof where it exists; price where the brief has one; the doubts answered; the action again.

- When the page answers an ad, email, or post, the first screen repeats that promise in the same words. A near-synonym is a miss. If the business cannot keep the promise, say so and stop; do not soften the headline to fit.
- Problem: write a scene or the buyer's own sentences, not a list of abstract pains. Quantify only with supplied totals.
- How it works: at most four steps (heuristic default), each a plain verb and what the reader gets. Name each step as the buyer's job ("We chase the late ones"), not as a feature name. A supplied method may get one short name the reader can repeat; never name a method the brief does not supply.
- Show one supplied use as a walkthrough or image sequence when the brief supplies the material.
- For an enterprise buyer, add a security or compliance block and a value line, each only from supplied certifications or totals.
- Price, when supplied: put any time the buyer must commit beside it. Strike a price through only with a reason beside it. Show pay-in-full beside a payment plan. Restate a supplied guarantee beside the price. Show a per-period price only beside the supplied total. Recommend a plan only when the brief names it. Add no reference price, decoy tier, pre-selected plan, or plan the brief does not supply.
- Doubts: three to five questions (heuristic default) the brief or buyer sources raise, in the buyer's words, including the blunt ones. Answer each with the direct answer, plus the supplied reason and edge case; leave out any part the brief does not supply. An answer that only repeats the question as a statement is cut. Name the buyer's current alternative with its supplied cost.
- One honest "not for you if" line, written from a supplied limit. Ask for it in Next only when the brief supplies no limit; do not leave a slot.
- Page title and meta description, when the copy is for a web page, from the same facts. Check their length limits in current search-engine documentation; if you cannot read it, say the limits are unchecked.

## Homepage

Job: several kinds of visitor each find their path. It is not a landing page.

- Lead with what the company does for its main buyer, in one sentence a stranger can repeat.
- Give each named visitor type one short block and one link. Name a type only when the brief names it.
- One primary action for the main buyer; every other path is a link.
- Do not stack every feature. The homepage routes; the deeper pages sell.

## Pricing page

Job: a reader who wants the product picks a plan without contacting anyone.

- Under each supplied plan name, one line saying who the plan is for, from the brief.
- State what every plan includes once, below the plans, not three times inside them.
- State the billing period, renewal, and cancellation terms as supplied. A missing term the buyer needs to pay is a placeholder.
- Answer the pricing questions the brief raises: what counts as a unit, what happens at the limit, how to change plans.
- No humor among the numbers. No invented comparison, anchor, or badge. One badge at most (heuristic default), and only on a plan the brief recommends.
- When the owner publishes no price, describe how pricing works ("priced after we see the job, quoted in writing before we start"). Never invent a range.

## Feature page

Job: a reader who knows the product learns what one part does for them.

- Chain each feature to its effect: what it is, what it changes for the reader, what that is worth in time, money, or worry. Stop the chain at the last link the brief supports.
- Show one use, in a real situation from the brief, before listing options.
- State the limit of the feature. End with the path to try it.

## About page

Job: a reader deciding whether to trust the company learns who is behind it and why.

- Open with the founder's moment or the company's stance, as supplied, not with a founding year or a mission statement.
- Order a founder story: the struggle, then why the founder is credible, then what exists now.
- Tie the story to what the buyer gets. An about page that never mentions the customer is a diary.
- Name people, numbers, and places only as supplied. Keep one action at the end.
- This is the page where personality has the most room. See [humor.md](humor.md).

## Comparison page

Job: a reader choosing between this product and a named alternative decides.

- Write only from supplied comparison facts, each with its source and check date. A competitor fact without both is a placeholder or is left out.
- Say what the alternative does well and who should choose it. A page that admits nothing is not believed.
- Compare on the criteria the buyer uses, from buyer sources, not on the criteria this product wins. Give each criterion a short paragraph: what each option does, and when the difference matters to the buyer.
- Comparing with the status quo (a spreadsheet, a manual process, doing nothing) needs no competitor facts and is often the truer comparison.
- Open with two or three sentences (heuristic default) on the main difference, then a table of the supplied facts. A table cell holds one fact with its source and date, not a tick or a cross. Write "not listed on [supplied source], as of [date]" for an absence unless a supplied docs page or trial confirms it.
- Add pricing only as supplied prices with their date and currency. A total for one stated team size may show the arithmetic; list the add-ons the source names.
- Add a switching section only from supplied migration facts: what transfers, what needs rework, and the support offered, each dated.
- Name only the competitors the brief names. If it names none, compare with the supplied status quo and ask for a competitor in Next; with no status quo either, write the landing page the facts carry, say so in line 1, and ask for a competitor in Next.
- Alternatives list: name only the alternatives the brief supplies, four to seven (heuristic default) when there are that many. Give the criteria first, then a recommendation for each use case.
- A page comparing two competitors: cover both on the same criteria and name who each suits. Add the product as a third option only where it fits the buyer.

## Launch or announcement page

Job: a reader learns what is new and takes the launch action.

- First line: what shipped and for whom. Not the company's excitement.
- Then what the reader can do now that they could not do before, shown in one use.
- A deadline or launch price needs its date, time, time zone, and reason, all supplied. An invented deadline is a lie.
- Importance words ("pivotal", "redefines", "revolutionary") are cut. State the fact and let the reader judge.

## Long sales page

Job: a reader with many doubts about a considered purchase decides on the page.

Shape: the outcome; the problem as lived; why the usual fixes fail; the method in named steps; proof; what is included; price and its reason; guarantee as supplied; doubts; who it is not for; the action.

- Length follows awareness: a reader who does not yet know the problem needs the long version; a reader ready to buy needs the offer first.
- Each item in an offer list has a name, a one-line benefit, and a value only when the brief supplies a price actually charged. Never total invented values.
- A bonus earns its place by removing one named doubt.
- Name the problem in the buyer's words from a supplied source. If the brief states a cause the buyer's words do not mention, name that cause.
- Answer the doubts the material supports: price, refund or cancellation terms as supplied, who it is not for, and the obvious alternative. Each answer uses supplied facts only.
- Open a question only if a later line answers it; close small ones within a few paragraphs. A tease never paid costs trust.
- Use it as a completeness check: does the page open, describe, argue, give an honest reason to act, lower the risk, and ask?

## Local service page

Job: a nearby reader with a job to be done calls or books.

- The first screen answers, in order: do you do this job, here, now, for roughly what, and can I trust you. The headline names the service and the area in plain words.
- For a page about one town, open with an answer that holds one supplied fact true only in that town; when the brief supplies none, ask for one in Next and add no local detail. Cut any paragraph that would fit another trade.
- The phone number or booking action is the largest thing on the first screen. Offer a quieter second path for readers who cannot call.
- Claims every local competitor makes ("family owned", "free estimates", "fast and reliable") go in one small line under the button, never in the headline.
- A reason to act today must be specific and true ("Call by noon, fixed today"), and only when the business can keep it.
- State after-hours handling honestly: when someone calls back, not "24/7" over a voicemail.
- Emergency jobs get one screen. Planned jobs get the method, the warranty, and the doubts.
- When the owner gives no exact price, use a supplied range and the factor that moves it, or describe the pricing process, as supplied, in one sentence. Never write a range the owner did not supply.

## Waitlist page

Job: a reader gives an address for something that does not exist yet.

- Say what it will be, for whom, and what joining gets them, as supplied. Say what is not known yet.
- No launch date, price, or feature the brief does not state. No invented queue position or count.

## Tagline

Job: a handful of words that make the product's one idea repeatable.

- Start from the concept, not from wordplay. A tagline is the concept at its shortest.
- Write at least ten, from at least three different concepts, then cut to three and pick one (heuristic default). The first three written are the ones everyone writes.
- Keep it short enough to say in one breath, about two to seven words (heuristic default).
- Test each: the swap test, the friend test, and reading it under the product name with no other text.
- A tagline that states a fact needs the fact supplied. "Hard to cut" on a lock tested once is a claim; "Heavy on purpose" is a supplied trait.
- Avoid the two-beat formula of two short parallel phrases unless the second phrase adds a new fact. It is the default shape of generated taglines.
- Do not claim a tagline is available to use as a trademark. Say that availability is unchecked.

## Product name candidates

Job: a short list of names the owner can check.

- Give each candidate with the concept it carries and how it sounds said aloud and spelled over the phone.
- Offer different kinds: a plain descriptive name, a real word used sideways, a compound, a coined word.
- State that trademark, domain, and meaning in other languages are unchecked. Never state that a name is free.

## Product description

Job: a shopper deciding on one item learns what it is, why it is this way, and whether it fits them.

- Open with the trait that makes this item itself, usually from the material list, not with the category.
- Then the facts a buyer checks: size, weight, material, what is in the box, what it fits. Exact figures, as supplied.
- Turn a known objection into the reason: say the flaw, then why it is there.
- State what it does not do or include when a buyer would assume otherwise.
- One short paragraph and a facts list beat three paragraphs of adjectives. Sensory words only when the brief supplies the sensation.

## Store or app listing text

Job: a browser decides to install or open the listing.

- The first sentence must work alone; most readers see only it.
- Lead with the main use in the buyer's words, then the next two or three uses (heuristic default) as short lines.
- State the price model and what is free, as supplied.
- Check each field's current length limit and content rules in the store's own documentation; if you cannot read it, say the limits are unchecked. Do not mention rankings, awards, or other stores unless supplied.

## Packaging or label copy

Job: a few lines read in the hand. This section also covers an insert or slip read after purchase.

- An insert speaks to a person who already owns the product. It carries no pre-sale question or selling line; it says what the owner holds and, when the brief supplies it, what happens next and by when.
- Decide who holds the pack and when: the buyer on a shelf, the buyer at home, or a person who received it as a gift. When supplied material shows the product is also bought for someone else, the pack may speak to the person who opens it; keep that evidence at its grade.
- One idea on the front: the trait that makes this item itself, stated as the trait, with no "only", "first", or comparison the brief does not supply. A front that only names the category is wasted.
- The front takes the register of the owner's supplied sample lines; a single dry line has room here when the humor gate passes. Facts the buyer needs go on the back, exact and plain.
- Regulated text (ingredients, warnings, claims about health) is written literally and is the owner's legal responsibility; say that it is unchecked.

## Case card

Job: a short proof story.

- Headline it with the starting number and the ending number, each with unit and period, all supplied.
- Order: the situation before, what they did, the result, the time it took, the customer's own words. Never fill a missing part. A takeaway closes it only when the source states one.

## Bio

Job: a reader learns why to listen to this person.

- First sentence: what they do now and for whom. Then one specific, supplied fact that earns trust. Then one human detail, when supplied.
- Third person for a page, first person for a profile, unless the owner says otherwise. Cut every adjective the person would not say about themselves aloud.

## Thank-you page

Job: a reader who just acted knows it worked and what happens next.

- Confirm the action in the first line. Then what happens next and when, from the brief. Then one optional thing to do meanwhile.
- No new sale that competes with the step just taken. One light line is welcome here when the voice allows it.
